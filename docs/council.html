Meet a sample council

How a claim gets measured before it ships — told through one real review.

What a council is, in plain words

A council is a review panel. Instead of one reviewer giving a change a quick once-over, we give the same change to several reviewers at once — and we give each reviewer, called a seat, one angle to attack from. One seat asks what could go wrong in real use. Another only checks whether the numbers add up. Because each seat looks for a different kind of problem, a problem has to slip past all of them to slip through.

We use a council for our riskiest changes — for example, changes to our licensing and provenance records. Provenance means the record of where something came from, who made it, and what license (legal permission) covers it.

Who sits on the council

SeatJob
Chair Runs the panel, keeps the record, and can veto — stop the change outright, even if the vote passes.
Seat A — Scenarios Asks: how could this go wrong in real use?
Seat B — Verification contracts Measures the claims. A verification contract is a check written down in advance — what will be measured, and what result counts as a pass.
Seat C — Adversarial Skeptic Assumes the change is broken and tries to prove it. Adversarial simply means taking the opposing side on purpose.
Seat D — Historian Checks the change against past decisions, so we do not quietly contradict something we settled earlier.

The pass rule: at least 3 of the 4 seats (A, B, C, and D) must support the change, and the Chair must not veto it. Support from 2 seats is not enough. A veto on its own is enough to stop it.

And one more checker, who is not on the council. The Reality Checker is a separate, independent checker — not a council seat, and not part of the vote. The Reality Checker trusts only evidence that has been re-measured first-hand, and starts every review at NEEDS WORK. Only fresh measurements can move that verdict to READY.

The worked example

Adapted from a real council review held on this project in October 2026. Wording is simplified for beginners; the votes, findings, and measurements are the real ones.

Act 1 — The proposal

We proposed a change — a pull request, which is a packaged set of edits offered for review before it becomes part of the project — that rewrote four of our own files into the collective voice. That means writing as “we” and “the maintainers”, instead of naming an individual.

The proposal was small on purpose: 15 content lines changed, across 4 files. Two of those files were our manifest — the record, kept in two matching formats, that lists every entry in this library with its author, source, and license evidence.

Act 2 — The vote fails

Result: FAIL — support 1 of 4.

Only the Historian (Seat D) supported the change as it stood. The other three seats opposed it. Under the pass rule — at least 3 of 4, and no Chair veto — the change could not go ahead. The Chair did not need to use a veto; the vote itself failed it.

The seats agreed on the facts. What they found was this:

  1. The claimed size was not the real size. Every line of text in a file ends with an invisible marker, called a line ending. The manifest spreadsheet (the comma-separated version of the manifest) had its line endings silently flipped from one style to another, on every line. No words changed — but the comparison tool (the diff, the list of lines a tool shows as added and removed between two versions) counts a flipped marker as a changed line. Measured: the real diff showed 153 lines changed, not the claimed 15.
  2. A fifth file was left contradicting the change. The proposal covered four files. A fifth file, in one of our packs, still used the old wording for the very same entries the proposal rewrote — so two parts of the project would have told two different stories. Measured: 8 leftover occurrences in that fifth file.
  3. A gate the proposal set for itself had not been met. The change included new wording for who holds the copyright (the legal right to control copying) in our material. The proposal itself said the owner had to confirm that exact wording before the change could go ahead — and that confirmation had not happened yet. Measured: the gate was written into the proposal, and it was still unmet.
  4. Two cells repeated a decision we had already made. In a spreadsheet, a cell is one box where a row and a column meet. Two cells in the manifest still said, in effect, “license undecided” about material that our NOTICE file — the file that states what our license covers — had already licensed under Apache-2.0, a well-known open license. Measured: 2 cells, saying undecided about something already decided.

One honest disagreement, kept on the record: the seats did not agree on how serious the first finding was. The Historian called the line-ending flip minor — a mechanical extra, with the content itself correct. Two other seats called it blocking — serious enough, on its own, to stop the change. We record disagreements like this instead of smoothing them over, because the size of a problem is sometimes a judgement call, and the person deciding deserves to see both calls.

Act 3 — The fix, and the pass

A failed vote is not the end of a change. It is a list of what to fix. Here, the list was short:

  1. The owner confirmed the wording. The owner confirmed the copyright-holder wording, and authorised the fix round — the fixes below.
  2. Line endings restored. The manifest spreadsheet was put back to its original line-ending style, with no words changed. Measured: the diff on that file came back to 4 lines — the real content change, and nothing else.
  3. The fifth file was fixed. The same collective-voice wording was applied there too, so it no longer contradicted the other files.
  4. The two cells were corrected. Both cells were updated to point to the Apache-2.0 license already recorded in the NOTICE file — and corrected in both manifest files, which were kept in sync, cell for cell.

Then the council stepped aside, and the separate Reality Checker re-measured the fixed files from the raw bytes — a byte is the smallest unit of stored text, and measuring raw bytes means trusting the file itself, not anyone’s summary of it:

Manifest spreadsheet: 33,070 bytes · 142 CRLF line endings · 0 leftover.

Here, CRLF is the name of the original line-ending style — two characters, carriage return plus line feed — and “0 leftover” covers both checks the checker ran: 0 bare LF line endings (the flipped style) remained, and 0 leftover occurrences of the old wording remained across all five files.

Reality Checker verdict: READY.

Even then, nothing shipped on the checker’s word alone. The change was merged — combined into the main copy of the project — only on the owner’s word. A council can pass a change, and a checker can call it ready, but the final say on an irreversible step (one that is hard to undo) stays with a person.

What this means for you

Councils exist so a claim gets measured before it ships. “15 lines changed” sounded small and tidy — and the content really was 15 lines — but measuring turned up a 153-line diff, a forgotten fifth file, an unconfirmed gate, and two stale cells.

A FAIL is the system working, not the system failing you. Nothing here was a disaster, and that is the point: every fix was small once the evidence was on the table. Catching a small problem in review is cheap. Catching it after it ships is not.

When you use this kit, expect the same treatment for your own changes — and welcome it. A panel that can say no, a checker who starts at NEEDS WORK, and a record that keeps the disagreements: that is how a claim earns the right to ship.
